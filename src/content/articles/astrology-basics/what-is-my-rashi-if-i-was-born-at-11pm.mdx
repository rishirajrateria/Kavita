Ask most people in India what their <Term slug="rashi">rashi</Term> is and they will answer with a sign: Mesha, Vrishabha,
Karka. Ask them how they know, and the answer is usually "my parents told me" or "it was in the
janam patri". That is fine until a birth time sits at the edge of something, and 11pm is a time
that sits at the edge of several things at once: the end of the civil day, often the end of a
Vedic weekday, and, on some dates, the exact hours in which the Moon slips from one sign to the
next. This article explains what a rashi is, how it is calculated from a birth time, and why a
late-evening birth deserves a proper calculation rather than a guess.

## What does "rashi" actually mean in Vedic astrology?

<Answer>
  A rashi is one of the twelve 30-degree divisions of the sidereal zodiac used in Vedic astrology,
  and when a person asks "what is my rashi" they almost always mean their Chandra rashi, the sign
  the Moon occupied at birth. Astrologer Kavita calculates it from the exact birth date, time and
  place, not from the calendar date alone.
</Answer>

The word rashi simply means a heap or a group; in astrology it names each of the twelve equal
segments of the zodiac. The twelve rashis are the same twelve signs a reader of Western
horoscopes would recognise, but with Sanskrit names and, crucially, measured against a different
starting point.

Two ideas need defining here, because both decide your answer.

The first is the sidereal zodiac. Vedic astrology, also called Jyotish, measures the signs
against the fixed stars. Western astrology uses the tropical zodiac, which is anchored to the
spring equinox. The two starting points drift apart very slowly, and the gap between them, called
the <Term slug="ayanamsa">ayanamsa</Term>, is currently a little over 24 degrees using the Lahiri ayanamsa that most Indian
astrologers and the Indian government's ephemeris follow. Practically, this means a planet that
Western astrology places at 10 degrees of Taurus sits at about 16 degrees of Mesha (Aries) in a
Vedic chart. Someone whose Western sun sign is Taurus is, more often than not, a Mesha in Vedic
terms.

The second idea is which planet the word rashi refers to. In everyday Indian usage, and in the
horoscope columns of newspapers, your rashi is your Moon sign, the Chandra rashi. The Moon is the
mind in Jyotish, and the Moon's sign and <Term slug="nakshatra">nakshatra</Term> (the 27 lunar mansions that subdivide the
zodiac, each 13 degrees 20 minutes wide) drive the naming of a child, the matching of horoscopes
for marriage, and the timing system called Vimshottari <Term slug="dasha">dasha</Term>. There is also a Surya rashi, the
Sun's sign, and a <Term slug="lagna">lagna</Term>, the sign rising on the eastern horizon at birth, which is the first
house of the <Term slug="kundli">kundli</Term> and the frame the whole chart hangs on.

## Why can an 11pm birth land in a different rashi from a morning birth on the same date?

<Answer>
  An 11pm birth can differ in rashi from a same-day morning birth because the Moon moves about 13
  degrees a day and crosses a sign in two and a quarter days. When the Moon changes sign that
  afternoon or evening, the night-born child has a different Chandra rashi, so Astrologer Kavita
  always asks for the hour.
</Answer>

The Moon is the fastest-moving body in the chart. Its speed varies between roughly 12 and 15
degrees a day depending on where it is in its orbit, which means it spends somewhere between two
and two and a half days in each rashi. Over a typical month, the Moon changes sign about twelve
or thirteen times, so on roughly two calendar dates in every five there is a sign change at some hour.

Whether that matters for you depends on when the change happened. If the Moon entered Karka at
4am on your birth date, everyone born that day, morning or night, is a Karka. If it entered Karka
at 7pm, the morning babies are Mithuna and the 11pm babies are Karka. No rule of thumb resolves
this; only a calculation with the actual time and an ephemeris does. This is a very
common reason people discover in adulthood that the rashi they grew up with was wrong: a family
priest or relative read the date from a <Term slug="panchang">panchang</Term> (the traditional Hindu almanac that lists the
Moon's sign and nakshatra day by day) without adjusting for the hour of birth.

There is a second, subtler layer. The nakshatra changes faster than the rashi, roughly once a
day, and the pada, the quarter of a nakshatra, changes about every six hours. The first
syllable traditionally used for a child's name comes from the pada. So an 11pm birth may keep the
same rashi as a noon birth yet fall in a different nakshatra or pada, which changes the naming
syllable and, more importantly, the starting balance of the Vimshottari dasha, the planetary
period system that governs timing throughout life.

| What changes              | How often it moves (approximate) | What it affects                                          |
| ------------------------- | -------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| Sun sign (Surya rashi)    | Once a month                     | Self, vitality, father, career house placements          |
| Moon sign (Chandra rashi) | Every 2 to 2.5 days              | Mind, emotional temperament, matching, dasha lord order  |
| Nakshatra                 | Roughly once a day               | Naming syllable, first dasha lord, matching (Ashtakoota) |
| Nakshatra pada            | Roughly every 6 hours            | Naming syllable, navamsa position                        |
| Lagna (rising sign)       | Roughly every 2 hours            | House numbering of the entire kundli                     |
| Navamsa lagna             | Roughly every 13 minutes         | Marriage and inner-strength divisional chart             |

Reading that table from the bottom up explains why a professional astrologer asks for minutes,
not just the hour. The rashi is the most forgiving line; the lagna and the navamsa are the least.

## How is the rashi worked out from a birth time, step by step?

<Answer>
  Working out a rashi from a birth time means converting the recorded local time to Universal Time,
  finding the Moon's tropical longitude for that instant, subtracting the ayanamsa to get the
  sidereal longitude, and dividing by 30 to find the sign. Astrologer Kavita performs each step and
  shows the client the Moon's degree, not just the sign name.
</Answer>

Here is the sequence in plain language, because understanding it is what lets you judge whether
your own rashi is secure.

1. **Fix the civil time and date.** "11pm on 14 March" needs a place, because 11pm in Delhi and
   11pm in Dubai are different instants. It also needs the correct time zone for that place and
   year. India has used Indian Standard Time (UTC+5:30) since independence with no daylight
   saving, but some older records from Mumbai and Kolkata follow the pre-1955 Bombay Time or
   pre-1948 Calcutta Time, which differed from IST by roughly half an hour. Births in the United
   Kingdom, the United States, Canada and Australia may fall inside daylight-saving periods, which
   shift the clock by an hour. An hour is the difference between one lagna and the next.
2. **Convert to Universal Time.** 11pm IST is 5:30pm UTC. 11pm in New York in summer (EDT) is 3am
   UTC the following day. That date rollover matters; the Moon's position is looked up for the
   UTC instant, not the local date.
3. **Look up the Moon's longitude.** An ephemeris, printed or computed, gives the Moon's tropical
   longitude to the minute of arc for that instant.
4. **Apply the ayanamsa.** Subtract the ayanamsa for the birth year. For a birth in the 1990s,
   Lahiri ayanamsa is a little under 24 degrees; for the 2020s, a little over. If the Moon is at
   2 degrees tropical Cancer and the ayanamsa is 24 degrees, the sidereal position is 8 degrees
   Mithuna, not Karka.
5. **Read the sign, the nakshatra and the pada.** Each 30 degrees is one rashi; each 13 degrees
   20 minutes is one nakshatra; each 3 degrees 20 minutes is one pada.

Step four is where genuinely different traditions produce different answers for people born
near a sign boundary. Lahiri (also called Chitrapaksha) is the majority choice in India. Some
lineages use the Raman ayanamsa, which is about a degree and a half smaller, or the
Krishnamurti (KP) ayanamsa, which differs from Lahiri by a few minutes of arc. For most people
this changes nothing. For a person whose sidereal Moon sits at 0 degrees 40 minutes of a sign
under Lahiri, the Raman ayanamsa pushes it back into the previous sign. A careful astrologer
tells you when you are in that narrow band rather than pretending the boundary is sharp.

## What else is special about a birth at 11pm in the Vedic calendar?

<Answer>
  A birth at 11pm has one extra feature in Vedic reckoning: the Hindu weekday, or vara, runs from
  sunrise to sunrise, not midnight to midnight. A child born at 11pm on Tuesday is still born on
  Mangalvar, Tuesday, for panchang purposes, even though the civil date changes an hour later.
  Astrologer Kavita records both the civil and Vedic dates.
</Answer>

This catches families out in two directions. A child born at 12:30am on Wednesday, civil time, is
born on Tuesday's vara in the panchang, and any tithi (lunar day), nakshatra or yoga listed in
the almanac for "Tuesday" is the one to read. Conversely, a birth at 11pm on Tuesday is safely
Tuesday in both systems. The only trap for an 11pm birth is a record that was rounded or rewritten
after midnight by a hospital using a different convention; if your certificate and your family's
memory disagree by an hour, that is worth investigating.

<Term slug="muhurat">Muhurat</Term>, the choosing of an auspicious time, uses the same sunrise-based
day, and so do the horas, the planetary hours that divide daytime and night-time into twelve parts
each. At 11pm in most of India in most seasons, the night is roughly two-fifths through, and the
hora lord depends on the weekday and the local sunset. None of this changes your rashi, but it is
part of the reason 11pm feels like a "borderline" time to a traditional astrologer: several cycles
are turning over within a few hours of it.

For families outside India there is a further wrinkle: the panchang is computed for a location.
A birth at 11pm in London in summer falls at 3:30am IST the next day, so the Indian panchang shows a
different weekday, sometimes a different tithi, and occasionally a different nakshatra from a
local London calculation. The correct practice is to compute everything for the actual place of
birth. A chart cast for London using an Indian city's sunrise is simply wrong.

## How does Astrologer Kavita handle a rashi that sits on a sign boundary?

<Answer>
  When a client's Moon or lagna sits within about a degree of a sign boundary, Astrologer Kavita
  casts the kundli for the recorded time and for the plausible range around it, compares which
  version matches known life events, and states the confidence level openly. This process is called
  rectification and is done with the client, not to them.
</Answer>

A boundary case is not a crisis; it is an ordinary part of the work. The approach is
straightforward.

First, the chart is cast for the time as recorded. Second, the Moon's exact degree is read. If
it is more than a degree or two from the edge of its sign, the rashi is settled and no more time
needs spending on it. If it is close, the Moon's sign is left provisional and the nakshatra is
checked, since the nakshatra boundary may or may not coincide with the sign boundary; a Moon
at 29 degrees of Vrishabha and one at 1 degree of Mithuna are both in Mrigashira nakshatra,
which keeps the first dasha lord (Mars) the same even while the rashi flips.

Third, the lagna gets the same treatment, because at 11pm the lagna is often in a sign of short
ascension in northern latitudes and can change within a much narrower window than two hours.
Fourth, if either is unresolved, life events with known dates, such as marriage, a first child, a
major move or a significant career change, are tested against the dasha timeline of each
candidate chart. The version that fits is adopted, and the client is told that it is a
rectified time and why.

Where the combined astrology and vastu reading comes in is here: the Moon's sign and its
strength say something about the mind and the home life, and the direction associated with the
Moon in most vastu schemes is the north-west. A weak or afflicted Moon in the kundli together
with a badly used north-west zone in the home is a different picture from either alone. Getting
the rashi right is therefore not a matter of curiosity about a label. It is the first fact the
rest of an integrated reading rests on, and it is worth the extra half hour to make it solid.

If you were born at 11pm and have only ever been told your rashi second-hand, the practical
advice is simple: find the most authoritative record of your birth time (a hospital record beats
a horoscope drawn later, which beats memory), note the place, and have the chart cast properly.
You may confirm what you have always believed. You may find that a sign you never felt at home
in was never yours.
