A <Term slug="kundli">kundli</Term>, the Vedic birth chart, is a still photograph of the sky. It shows what was where at
the moment of birth, and from it an astrologer reads temperament, strengths, vulnerabilities and
the general shape of a life. What the photograph cannot show on its own is when. When does the
career theme in the tenth house come forward? When does the strong Jupiter that promises support
actually deliver it? When is a marriage more likely, a move, a period of health to guard?

The <Term slug="dasha">dasha</Term> system is Vedic astrology's answer. Dasha means a period or a state, and the dasha
system is a way of unrolling the birth chart across time, so that different planets take turns
to lead. Without it the kundli describes; with it the kundli schedules. This article explains the
most widely used system, Vimshottari dasha, how it is calculated, what it can and cannot tell
you, and why it is the bridge between a horoscope and a vastu correction.

## What is a dasha in Vedic astrology?

<Answer>
  A dasha in Vedic astrology is a planetary period: a stretch of time during which one planet is
  considered the principal influence on a person's life, bringing forward whatever that planet
  signifies in the birth chart. The Vimshottari dasha system, which Astrologer Kavita uses as the
  primary timing tool, assigns the nine planets fixed periods that together span 120 years.
</Answer>

The idea is simpler than the Sanskrit suggests. Each planet in a kundli has a set of meanings,
partly fixed by tradition (Jupiter for wisdom, children and teachers; Saturn for discipline,
delay and long labour; Venus for relationships, comfort and the arts) and partly fixed by the
chart itself, through the houses the planet rules and occupies. A dasha is the period in which
that planet's portfolio is opened. During a Saturn dasha, Saturn's themes and Saturn's houses
are where life is being lived. When the dasha passes to Mercury, the emphasis moves.

The system is layered. The largest unit is the <Term slug="mahadasha">mahadasha</Term>, the major period, ruled by one planet
for between six and twenty years. Within each mahadasha run nine <Term slug="antardasha">antardashas</Term>, also called
bhuktis, sub-periods ruled by each planet in turn. Within each antardasha are nine
pratyantardashas, and the subdivision can continue, though most working astrologers stop at
the third level. An astrologer describing "Saturn-Venus" means the Venus antardasha inside
the Saturn mahadasha, and both planets are read together.

Vimshottari means "of 120", the total years in one full cycle. Nobody expects to live through
the whole cycle; what matters is where in the cycle a person is born and which periods fall
within a lifetime.

| Order | Planet (Sanskrit) | Mahadasha length | Nakshatras ruled                          |
| ----- | ----------------- | ---------------- | ----------------------------------------- |
| 1     | Ketu              | 7 years          | Ashwini, Magha, Mula                      |
| 2     | Venus (Shukra)    | 20 years         | Bharani, Purva Phalguni, Purva Ashadha    |
| 3     | Sun (Surya)       | 6 years          | Krittika, Uttara Phalguni, Uttara Ashadha |
| 4     | Moon (Chandra)    | 10 years         | Rohini, Hasta, Shravana                   |
| 5     | Mars (Mangala)    | 7 years          | Mrigashira, Chitra, Dhanishta             |
| 6     | Rahu              | 18 years         | Ardra, Swati, Shatabhisha                 |
| 7     | Jupiter (Guru)    | 16 years         | Punarvasu, Vishakha, Purva Bhadrapada     |
| 8     | Saturn (Shani)    | 19 years         | Pushya, Anuradha, Uttara Bhadrapada       |
| 9     | Mercury (Budha)   | 17 years         | Ashlesha, Jyeshtha, Revati                |
|       | **Total**         | **120 years**    | 27 nakshatras                             |

The sequence always runs in this order and then repeats. Someone born into a Jupiter mahadasha
will pass through Saturn, Mercury, Ketu, Venus, Sun and so on in that fixed succession.

## How is the Vimshottari dasha calculated from the birth chart?

<Answer>
  The Vimshottari dasha is calculated from the Moon's exact position at birth. The nakshatra, or
  lunar mansion, that the Moon occupies names the first mahadasha lord, and the fraction of the
  nakshatra the Moon has crossed is subtracted from that lord's full period to give the balance
  remaining at birth. Astrologer Kavita computes this from the birth time and place.
</Answer>

The zodiac of 360 degrees is divided into 27 <Term slug="nakshatra">nakshatras</Term> of 13 degrees 20 minutes each. Each
nakshatra is assigned to one of the nine dasha lords in the sequence above, and the assignment
repeats three times around the zodiac, which is why each planet rules three nakshatras. Find
the Moon, find its nakshatra, and you have the planet whose mahadasha was running at birth.

The second step is the balance. If the Moon is at the very start of a nakshatra, the whole
mahadasha lies ahead. If it is three-quarters of the way through, only a quarter of that
period remains. Take Bharani, a Venus-ruled nakshatra that runs from 13 degrees 20 minutes to
26 degrees 40 minutes of Mesha (Aries). A Moon at 16 degrees 40 minutes of Mesha is 3 degrees
20 minutes into Bharani, exactly one quarter of the way through. One quarter of Venus's 20 years
has therefore elapsed, and the child is born with 15 years of Venus dasha remaining. Venus ends at age 15,
Sun runs from 15 to 21, Moon from 21 to 31, Mars from 31 to 38, Rahu from 38 to 56, Jupiter
from 56 to 72, Saturn from 72 to 91, and so on.

Antardashas are proportional. The length of an antardasha equals the mahadasha length
multiplied by the antardasha lord's own period, divided by 120. The first antardasha in any
mahadasha is ruled by the mahadasha lord itself, and the rest follow in sequence.

| Mahadasha         | First antardasha (same lord)      | Longest antardasha inside it   | Shortest antardasha inside it |
| ----------------- | --------------------------------- | ------------------------------ | ----------------------------- |
| Sun (6 years)     | Sun-Sun: 6 × 6 ÷ 120 = 3.6 months | Sun-Venus: 12 months           | Sun-Sun: 3.6 months           |
| Moon (10 years)   | Moon-Moon: 10 months              | Moon-Venus: 20 months          | Moon-Sun: 6 months            |
| Venus (20 years)  | Venus-Venus: 3 years 4 months     | Venus-Venus: 3 years 4 months  | Venus-Sun: 1 year             |
| Saturn (19 years) | Saturn-Saturn: just over 3 years  | Saturn-Venus: 3 years 2 months | Saturn-Sun: 11.4 months       |

These figures are arithmetic, not opinion, and any two competent astrologers will produce the
same table. Where they differ is in the inputs: the birth time, which fixes the Moon's degree;
the <Term slug="ayanamsa">ayanamsa</Term>, the correction between the tropical zodiac and the sidereal zodiac that Vedic
astrology uses, which shifts every planet by the same amount; and the length of the year
assumed, since some use a 365.25-day year and others a 360-day "savana" year. The last of these
alone can move a dasha boundary by several months in later life.

The sensitivity to birth time is worth quantifying, because it explains why the exact time
matters even when the rashi is not in doubt. The Moon moves about 13 degrees a day, roughly
0.55 degrees an hour. One hour of error is therefore about 4 per cent of a nakshatra. In a
Venus-ruled nakshatra, 4 per cent of 20 years is about ten months; in a Ketu-ruled nakshatra, 4
per cent of 7 years is about three and a half months. A birth time recorded an hour wrong will
put every dasha boundary in that person's life out by that amount. This is one of the ways
birth-time rectification works: known events are tested against candidate dasha timelines
until the boundaries fit.

## Why does the same kundli read differently in different years?

<Answer>
  The same kundli reads differently in different years because the dasha changes which planet leads.
  A chart's Saturn placement is a background fact during a Venus mahadasha and the main event during
  a Saturn mahadasha. Astrologer Kavita reads a client's current mahadasha and antardasha alongside
  the transits, so the advice is for this period of life, not a lifetime average.
</Answer>

Two people can have a similarly placed Mars and experience it completely differently, because
one is running Mars dasha at thirty and the other will not reach it until sixty-five. A well
placed Jupiter is a lifelong asset, but its mahadasha, sixteen years long, is the stretch in
which its gifts are most available. A poorly placed Saturn is a lifelong discipline, and its
nineteen-year mahadasha is when that discipline is enforced.

The tradition reads dasha and transit (<Term slug="gochar">gochar</Term>, the current movement of planets through the
sky) together, and neither alone. A famous example is <Term slug="sade-sati">Sade Sati</Term>, the roughly seven-and-a-half
year transit of Saturn through the sign before, the sign of, and the sign after the natal
Moon. Sade Sati falls on everyone about every thirty years; whether a particular Sade Sati is
demanding or merely busy depends, among other things, on which dasha is running underneath it.
Saturn transit during Saturn dasha is a different experience from Saturn transit during Venus
dasha. This is why generic newspaper descriptions of "Sade Sati for Makara rashi" can only ever
be approximate.

Three honest caveats belong here. First, a dasha describes themes, pace and emphasis; it does
not dictate specific outcomes. A Venus mahadasha is a period in which relationships, comfort,
beauty and Venus's houses are foregrounded; it is not a promise of marriage, and any astrologer
who converts it into one is overreaching. Second, the system is one of several. Vimshottari is
the most widely used and the one the tradition treats as general purpose, but the classical
texts also describe Ashtottari (108 years), Yogini (36 years), Kalachakra and the sign-based
Chara dasha of the Jaimini school, and some astrologers apply two systems and look for
agreement. Third, dasha analysis is interpretive. The planet's strength, its house, its
aspects, and its relationship with the antardasha lord all have to be weighed, and different
practitioners weigh them differently. A dasha reading is a considered opinion within a
tradition, and should be offered as such.

## How does dasha timing guide a vastu correction?

<Answer>
  Dasha timing guides a vastu correction by identifying which planet leads a person's life now, and
  therefore which direction of the home deserves attention now. In the most common scheme each
  planet governs a direction, so a running Saturn dasha draws attention to the west, a Jupiter dasha
  to the north-east. Astrologer Kavita uses the dasha to sequence corrections.
</Answer>

This is the practical heart of an integrated astrology and vastu reading, and it is what
neither discipline can do alone.

A vastu assessment of an existing home produces a list of departures from the ideal: a kitchen
in the north-east, a heavy store in the north, a master bedroom in the south-east. Left to
itself, vastu ranks these by textbook severity, which is a reasonable default but has no
knowledge of the people living there. Astrology, meanwhile, can say that a person's Venus is
weak and that they have just entered an eighteen-year Rahu mahadasha, but it cannot rearrange
a home.

Put the two together and priorities emerge. In the planet-direction scheme most widely used in
Indian practice, the Sun governs the east, Venus the south-east, Mars the south, Rahu the
south-west, Saturn the west, the Moon the north-west, Mercury the north and Jupiter the
north-east; Ketu is variously placed in the south-west with Rahu or at the centre, and schools
differ. A family entering a Rahu mahadasha with a south-west that is a hollow balcony or an
empty guest room has a clear first task: give the south-west the weight, solidity and daily
presence the tradition wants, ideally by making it the master bedroom. A person in a Mercury
antardasha who works from home benefits from the north being their study rather than their
storage. The kitchen in the north-east may still be the "bigger" textbook fault, but if
Jupiter is strong and its period is far off, it can wait its turn.

Dasha boundaries are also natural moments to act. The change of a mahadasha, which happens only
a handful of times in a life, is a change of season; many practitioners treat the first year of
a new mahadasha as the time its character declares itself. Families who know a boundary is
approaching can plan a move, a renovation, or simply a rearrangement of who sleeps where, to
coincide with it, in the same spirit in which one chooses a <Term slug="muhurat">muhurat</Term>, an auspicious time, for
beginning something important.

None of this requires believing that a bed moved to the south-west causes a promotion. It
requires only the modest claim on which the whole tradition rests: that a life has seasons, that
the seasons can be read from the birth chart with a fair degree of consistency, and that a home
arranged to support the season a person is in is better than one arranged at random. Knowing
your dasha is knowing what time it is. What you do with the time is, as always, up to you.
