Ask any Vedic astrologer for a reading and the first three things she will ask for are your date, place and time of birth. The date and place are usually easy. The time is the one that causes anxiety: many people know it only roughly, some know it only as a family story, and a few do not know it at all. This article explains, without mystique, why the time matters, which parts of a chart are sensitive to it, what can still be read when it is approximate or missing, and how to go about finding it.

## Why does the birth time matter so much in a kundli?

<Answer>
  A kundli — the Vedic birth chart — is a map of the sky at the moment and place of birth. The time
  fixes the ascendant (lagna), the sign rising on the eastern horizon, and the ascendant sets the
  twelve houses that Astrologer Kavita reads for every area of life, so it anchors the whole chart.
</Answer>

The <Term slug="kundli">kundli</Term> is not a general horoscope for a day or a month. It is a snapshot: the positions of the Sun, Moon and planets against the zodiac, seen from one point on the Earth's surface at one instant. The planets themselves move slowly enough that the date alone fixes most of them. What the time adds is the local horizon. As the Earth turns, a different part of the zodiac rises in the east every couple of hours, and the sign on the eastern horizon at the instant of birth is the <Term slug="lagna">lagna</Term>, or ascendant.

The lagna does a great deal of work. It is the starting point of the twelve <Term slug="bhava">bhavas</Term>, the houses, each of which governs a domain of life — self, wealth and speech, siblings and effort, home and mother, children and intellect, and so on round the chart. A planet's meaning depends on which house it occupies and which houses it rules, and both of those depend on the lagna. Change the lagna by one sign and every planet in the chart moves to a different house. That is why two people born on the same day in the same city, one at dawn and one at dusk, can have charts that read very differently even though their planets sit in the same signs.

The time also fixes the finer charts. Vedic astrology divides each sign into smaller segments to produce a family of divisional charts (vargas), of which the ninth division, the <Term slug="navamsa">navamsa</Term>, is the most used. These divisions are narrow, so the ascendant of a divisional chart can change within minutes. The more precise the time, the more of this detail can be trusted.

## Which parts of the chart change fastest?

<Answer>
  The slowest parts of a kundli are the outer planets, which barely move in a day; the fastest are
  the divisional-chart ascendants, which can change in minutes. In between sit the Moon's sign, its
  lunar mansion and the main ascendant. Astrologer Kavita uses this ladder to judge how much of a
  chart a given birth time supports.
</Answer>

The table below lists the main elements of a chart in order of how sensitive they are to the clock. The intervals are typical rather than exact: the rate at which signs rise varies with latitude and with the sign itself, and the Moon's speed varies through the month.

| Element of the chart                             | Changes roughly every        | What a small error in the time does                                   |
| ------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Sun, Jupiter, Saturn, Rahu and Ketu signs        | Weeks to years               | Almost nothing; the date is enough                                    |
| Mercury, Venus and Mars signs                    | Several weeks                | Almost nothing, except on a day the planet changes sign               |
| Moon's sign (rashi)                              | About two and a quarter days | Nothing on most days; on a sign-change day the time decides the rashi |
| Moon's lunar mansion (nakshatra)                 | About one day                | Matters on a mansion-change day, which sets the starting dasha        |
| Balance of the first dasha                       | Continuously                 | An hour's error can shift dasha boundaries by weeks or months         |
| Ascendant (lagna) sign                           | About two hours              | An error near a sign boundary changes every house                     |
| Navamsa (ninth-division) ascendant               | About thirteen minutes       | Changes the navamsa chart used for marriage and inner strength        |
| Higher divisional charts (D-10, D-60 and others) | A few minutes or less        | Usually unreliable unless the time is recorded to the minute          |

Two rows deserve a comment. The Moon's sign, the <Term slug="rashi">rashi</Term>, is what most Indian families mean when they say "my sign", and it is usually safe even with a rough time, because the Moon stays in a sign for more than two days. The Moon's lunar mansion, the <Term slug="nakshatra">nakshatra</Term>, matters more than people expect: the Moon's exact position within its nakshatra sets the starting point of the <Term slug="dasha">dasha</Term> system, the planetary periods that Vedic astrology uses to time events. A birth time that is off by an hour does not usually change the nakshatra, but it does move the dasha boundaries — the dates on which one period ends and the next begins — by an amount that grows with the length of the period.

## What if my birth time is only approximate?

<Answer>
  An approximate birth time still gives a useful reading, provided the astrologer says plainly what
  depends on it. Astrologer Kavita casts the chart for the best estimate, checks whether the
  ascendant or Moon sits near a boundary, and marks anything that would change with a different time
  as provisional rather than firm.
</Answer>

Most birth times are approximate in some degree, and the honest approach is to work with the uncertainty rather than pretend it away. In practice there are three questions to ask of an approximate time.

First, how wide is the window? "Around seven in the morning" from a mother who was present is a window of perhaps twenty minutes. "Morning" is a window of six hours. The first window will usually fix the lagna; the second may not.

Second, does the window cross a boundary? If the ascendant changes sign at 6:50 and the window is 6:40 to 7:00, the whole house structure of the chart is in doubt, and the reading has to consider both possibilities. If the ascendant changed at 5:30 and again at 7:40, the window sits comfortably inside one sign and the main chart is secure. The same check applies to the Moon on days when it changes sign or nakshatra.

Third, what is the reading for? A reading focused on temperament, strengths and the broad shape of life relies mostly on the slow elements and the lagna, and tolerates a wider window. A reading focused on the timing of a change — when a period of effort is likely to ease, which years favour a move — leans on the dasha boundaries and the divisional charts, and needs the time to be tighter. A good consultation matches the questions to what the data can support.

## What can be read with no birth time at all?

<Answer>
  Without any birth time, a chart can still be cast for the birth date, giving the Moon's sign and
  lunar mansion on most days and the positions of the slower planets. Astrologer Kavita reads such a
  chart for character and broad tendencies, but does not time events from it or claim precision the
  data cannot carry.
</Answer>

When no time is known, the usual practice is to cast a chart with the Moon as the reference point rather than the ascendant, sometimes called a chandra kundli or Moon chart. Because the Moon moves about one sign in two and a quarter days, its sign is fixed on most dates, and its nakshatra is fixed on most dates too. From these, the dasha sequence can be laid out, though its exact start dates remain uncertain within a day's movement of the Moon. The slower planets are all where the date puts them, so their relationships to one another — the <Term slug="yoga">yogas</Term>, or planetary combinations, that the tradition attaches meaning to — can be considered.

What cannot be done is anything that depends on the houses. Which house Saturn sits in, whether Jupiter aspects the seventh house, whether the tenth house of career is strong — these questions have no answer without a lagna. Some practitioners use a conventional time such as sunrise or noon in this situation. That produces a chart, but not the chart of the person, and it should be described as a convention rather than presented as the birth chart. A reading with no birth time is a reading of tendencies, and it is better to say so.

## Where can I find my exact birth time?

<Answer>
  The most reliable sources are a hospital record or birth certificate that records the time, a
  horoscope (janam patri) prepared soon after birth, and the memory of someone who was present.
  Astrologer Kavita asks clients to check more than one of these, because rounded or transcribed
  times are common.
</Answer>

Start with documents. Hospital discharge papers, the birth register of the hospital, and birth certificates issued by a municipal corporation or registrar sometimes record the time to the minute. Be aware that a suspiciously round figure — 10:00, 4:30 — may be a rounding by the person who wrote it down rather than the actual moment. The further back the birth, the more likely this is.

Next, look for a janam patri, the traditional handwritten or printed horoscope that many Indian families had prepared by a family astrologer or priest within days of a birth. These usually state the time, and because they were prepared close to the event they are often more precise than the memory of decades later. They also record the place, and sometimes the <Term slug="panchang">panchang</Term> details — the lunar day and nakshatra — which can be cross-checked against the stated time.

Then ask people. A mother, a grandmother, an aunt who was in the house — anyone who remembers the day may remember whether it was before or after a meal, before or after sunrise, during a particular television programme. These anchors are not exact, but they can confirm or contradict a document.

Finally, note the place and the clock that was in use. A chart is calculated from universal time, so the astrologer needs to know what the local clock meant. Within India today that is straightforward, but births in the mid-twentieth century may have been recorded on regional time before Indian Standard Time was universally adopted, and births during the Second World War fell under a temporary time change. Outside India, daylight-saving rules vary by country and by year. Give the place exactly and let the astrologer handle the conversion.

## What is birth-time rectification, and when is it worth doing?

<Answer>
  Birth-time rectification is the process of narrowing an uncertain birth time by testing candidate
  times against known events in a person's life. Astrologer Kavita treats it as a working estimate
  to be stated openly, useful when the window crosses a sign boundary, and not as a substitute for a
  recorded time.
</Answer>

Rectification works backwards. Instead of using the chart to describe life, it uses life to choose between charts. The astrologer takes the range of possible times, casts charts for each candidate lagna within it, and asks which chart best fits the dates of events the person already knows: a marriage, a move abroad, the birth of a child, a change of career, a serious illness of a parent. Because the dasha boundaries and the houses shift with the time, different candidates make different predictions about these past events, and the one whose predictions match best is taken as the working time.

The method is only as good as the events supplied and the judgement applied, and different traditions weigh the evidence differently. A rectified time is therefore an estimate with a stated confidence, not a fact. It is worth attempting when the uncertainty is large enough to matter — a window that crosses a lagna boundary, or a nakshatra boundary on the day of birth — and when there are several well-dated events to test against. It is not worth attempting to shave five minutes off a time that is already recorded, and an astrologer who "corrects" a documented time without strong reason should be asked why.

Some practitioners also use a prashna chart — a chart cast for the moment a question is asked — when the birth chart is unusable. That is a different technique with its own logic, and it is best described as such rather than folded into the birth chart.

## What should I tell Astrologer Kavita about my birth time?

<Answer>
  Tell Astrologer Kavita the time you have, where it came from, and how confident you are in it —
  for example "7:10 am from the hospital certificate" or "sometime after breakfast, from my mother".
  She casts the chart accordingly and says which parts of the reading are firm and which would
  change with a different time.
</Answer>

The most useful thing you can bring to a consultation is not a perfect time but an honest account of the time you have. State the figure, the source, and your confidence. If you have two sources that disagree, bring both; the disagreement itself is information. If you have no time at all, say so, and the reading will be planned around what the date alone can support.

The one thing to avoid is guessing a precise time to seem better prepared. A chart cast for a guessed minute looks exactly as detailed as a chart cast for a recorded one, and the detail is fictional. A reading that says "your ascendant is Leo, and everything that follows assumes it" is worth more than one that presents a guessed Virgo with unearned confidence. When the chart is read together with the vastu of your home, as it is in an integrated consultation, the home's orientation and layout do not depend on the birth time at all, so that half of the reading stays firm whatever the state of the birth record.
